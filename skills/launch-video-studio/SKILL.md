# Launch Video Studio

This is direction, not a recipe. Nothing here is a rule, a template or a required tool. It is a way of thinking about a motion piece, a set of questions that lead to better answers, and a bank of ideas to push off from. Take what serves the piece in front of you and ignore the rest. If you have a better idea than anything written here, that is the idea to make.

The only fixed point is honesty about the product, covered at the end.

## Aim higher than the request

"Make a video for my app" is usually answered with a slideshow: a logo, three feature captions, a call to action. The person asking almost always wanted more than they knew how to ask for.

So raise the brief yourself. Treat it as the piece you would put first on a showreel. Ask what a motion designer with a week and a point of view would make, then make that. The strongest results come from an ambitious, open brief and one specific idea, not from a long list of requirements.

Ambition is not more effects. It is one clear idea, executed with care in every frame.

## Start with what is true

Before inventing anything, find out what the thing actually is.

- What does it do, in the words its makers use? Read the landing page, the README, the store listing, the launch post.
- What does it look like in use? Screenshots, recordings, the live product.
- What is the one thing it does that nothing else does? Most products have exactly one. That is the video.
- Who is it for, and what were they doing the minute before they needed it?
- What does the brand already feel like: its colour, its type, its voice, its icon?

If the person points at work they admire, study the frames, not the description. Pull stills from it and write down what you see: what the stage is, how type is set, whether the interface is whole or in pieces, how one moment becomes the next, how dense a frame is. The craft is in the frames. Use what you learn as direction, not as something to copy.

## Find the idea

A video needs one idea you could say in a sentence. Some questions that tend to surface it:

- **What is the verb?** Sorts, remembers, shortens, finds, merges, protects, predicts. Animate the verb and the features explain themselves.
- **What is the before?** The mess, the wait, the repetition, the thing that got lost. A problem the viewer recognises without being told is the fastest way in.
- **What would this be if it were a physical object?** A ticket, a stack of cards, a key, a drawer, a dial. Objects have weight and edges, and they are more memorable than rectangles.
- **What is the smallest piece that carries the whole?** A single button, a search field, one row. A whole product can be told through one component changing state.
- **What is the single image someone would screenshot?**
- **What would happen with no words at all?** If the motion still tells the story, the idea is strong.

### Idea starters

These are sparks, collected from studying a lot of code-made motion work and from making it. Each one is a principle wearing an example. Steal the principle, change everything else, combine them, or let them remind you of something better.

**Turn the product into an object.** A flight-booking tool becomes a boarding pass tilting in space with real type on it. A notes app becomes a stack of index cards. A link shortener becomes a tape measure. The object can carry real product data on its surface.

**Show the ugly truth, with a number on it.** A raw, overlong URL types itself across a black screen while a counter in the corner climbs: 163 characters. No narration needed. Find the product's equivalent: the steps, the tabs, the minutes, the megabytes, shown plainly.

**Flood, then clear.** Start on one small item. Pull back to reveal hundreds of them on a tilted plane filling the frame, with a short line of type over the field. Then cut to white, and five of them sit in a calm row. Chaos to order is one of the most reliable shapes there is, and the pull-back is what sells the scale.

**One component, many lives.** A button says "Generate". It is pressed, tightens into a circle with a spinner, resolves to a check, stretches into a pill with a thumbnail, then opens into a full card with controls, and a cursor scrubs it. One object, continuous, morphing with blur on every change of shape. This can be an entire video.

**Let the type do it.** A sentence on an empty stage, typed with a caret or revealed a word at a time. Add one thing: small tags drifting in from the edges, a word swapped for an image, a line struck through and rewritten. Type-only pieces are underrated and hard to make badly.

**Put things inside the letters.** A row of faces fills the counter of a letterform. A product screenshot sits inside the bowl of a numeral. A pill of avatars replaces two letters of a word. Type and image sharing one shape always looks designed.

**Give it a hand.** Handwritten type that draws itself, a circle scrawled around the thing that matters, an arrow, an underline that wobbles. Warmth and imperfection next to precise interface is a strong contrast.

**Give it a character.** A small mascot, a pixel creature, a cursor with a personality that reacts to what happens. Even a few frames of acting make a piece feel authored.

**Fan the cards.** Results, applications, messages, options, as a deck that fans out, restacks, deals itself, with a count beside it. A deck shows quantity and choice at once.

**Scan it.** A beam passes over an image or document, regions light up, and what was recognised lifts off the surface and lands somewhere else as clean structured text. Good for anything that reads, parses, imports or understands.

**Filter it live.** A list that physically collapses as each character is typed, survivors sliding up to close the gaps. The consequence of every keystroke is visible.

**Gather and commit.** Scattered things are selected one by one, pull together into a stack, and drop into a single action that visibly presses.

**Press the real keys.** Large physical keycaps for the product's shortcut, pressed in sequence, and the world changes on the last one.

**Flip the world.** Dark for the problem, light for the product, and the change happens on an action, as a wipe that grows from the thing that caused it. Or the reverse. Or colour floods into black and white.

**Zoom through.** Push into a detail until it fills the frame and becomes the next scene: into a pixel, a letter, a button, an icon.

**Count it.** One number, set huge, moving from the old value to the new one. Give it time in proportion to the size of the change.

**Stagger a before and after.** Land the before and hold it. Then let the after arrive with force. One replacing the other is stronger than both appearing side by side.

**Draw the line.** A path draws itself between things and something travels along it. Integrations, pipelines, journeys, cause and effect.

**Fold the leftovers into a bento.** Everything that did not earn its own moment sits in a grid of small cards, each with one tiny motion of its own: a chip popping, a toggle sliding, a key pressing, a number ticking.

**Become the icon.** End by having something from the video (three rows, a stack, a shape) fold together and turn into the logo.

When the product's real mechanic is none of these, that is good news. Work out what the interaction physically does and build that. Those are the scenes people remember.

## Shape the story

There is no required structure. Some shapes to consider, and to break:

- **Problem, turn, proof, close.** The viewer's pain, the product arriving, the one thing it does best, how to get it. The classic, and it works.
- **One continuous transformation.** A single object or component that keeps changing, with no cuts.
- **One take.** A camera that travels through a space where each feature lives.
- **A list, escalating.** Three or five beats, each bigger than the last.
- **A single question, answered.**
- **A day, compressed.** Morning to night with the product in it.

Things that tend to hold whatever the shape:

- The opening seconds decide whether anyone stays. Lead with something that moves and means something. A logo on its own is neither.
- Time is attention. Give the most time to the thing that matters most. Evenly divided runtime is a feature tour.
- One thought per moment. If a frame needs two captions it is two moments.
- Many people will watch without sound and on a phone. The piece should read silent and small.
- End when you are done. A moment that sits with nothing happening after its payoff feels longer than it is.
- Sketch the beats in words before building: what is on screen, what moves, what it says. Show the person, then keep going. Words are cheap to change and renders are not.

Length follows the idea. A single morph might be eight seconds. A full launch piece often lands between twenty and forty.

## Choose a look

A look is a small set of decisions held consistently. Make them on purpose. Some axes:

**The stage.** What is behind everything? Flat warm off-white, pure black, a single saturated brand colour, a soft out-of-focus wash of one or two pale hues, a tinted field with a vignette. Quiet stages make detailed subjects look expensive.

**Colour.** How many, and what does each mean? One accent used only where the eye should go is a strong default, because restraint reads as confidence. But a piece built on a bold field of one colour, or on two colours trading places, can be just as controlled. Colour inside the product's own interface is a separate matter from colour on the stage.

**Type.** The pairing sets the tone faster than anything else. A tight grotesk with a serif italic for the emphasised word feels editorial. A serif headline with a small sans label feels literary. A mono face feels technical. A handwriting face feels human. One family in two weights can be enough. Decide how words arrive: all at once, a word at a time, typed, or masked up from a baseline.

**Surfaces and depth.** Are objects flat, softly shadowed, tilted in perspective? Layered soft shadows give weight on a light stage. A slight permanent tilt or slow drift keeps a held shot alive. Decide how physical this world is and keep it that physical throughout.

**Texture.** A little film grain, paper tooth, a soft light falloff. Small amounts separate "rendered" from "made".

**Composition.** Vary it. A centred column, then a left-right split, then one wide object, then a grid. Several moments composed the same way in a row start to feel like slides. Leave real empty space; a frame does not need to be full to be finished.

Let the brand decide where it clearly can. A developer tool can live entirely on black in a mono face. A writing tool can be warm paper and a serif. A playful consumer product can be loud. The look should feel like it belongs to this product and no other.

**What tends to read as generic**, so you can choose it knowingly or avoid it: glowing blue-purple gradients on dark, gradient-filled text, background grids and floating particles, glass panels, every element centred, every caption styled identically, the whole application window sitting on screen from start to finish, and scenes that simply crossfade.

## Showing the product

- **Whole or in pieces?** The complete interface is useful once, to say "this is the thing". After that, lifting out a single component, enlarging it and letting it perform alone is usually stronger: it is legible on a phone and it directs attention.
- **Footage or rebuilt?** Rebuilding the interface in code stays sharp at any size, can be choreographed exactly, and keeps private data out. Real footage proves the thing exists. Either can be right. Mixing them takes care.
- **Faithful where it counts.** When you rebuild, match what makes it recognisably theirs: proportions, corner radii, the selection colour, icon weight, the exact wording of labels.
- **Stage additions are welcome.** A beam, a highlight, something flying from one place to another: illustration that explains what the product is doing. Just be able to say afterwards which parts were illustration.
- **Invented content should look invented.** Demo data that an ordinary user might plausibly have, with no real people, keys, links or customers in it.

## Move things well

How it moves matters more than what moves.

- **Nothing travels at constant speed** unless it is drifting. Arrivals start fast and settle. Journeys between two resting places ease at both ends. Things with weight overshoot slightly and come back.
- **Arrive with more than opacity.** A small rise, a slight scale, a blur that clears. A fade alone looks like a presentation.
- **Stagger.** Siblings arrive a few frames apart, not together: rows, cards, words, letters. Simultaneity is the clearest tell of a template.
- **Change shape, do not swap.** When one state becomes another, morph it: width, radius, position, with a touch of blur across the change. Continuity of an object across states is what makes motion feel designed.
- **Blur the fast parts.** Motion blur, or a blur that peaks mid-move, turns a jump into a movement.
- **Make interaction physical.** A press compresses. A click leaves a mark and the consequence follows a moment later. A cursor arrives before it acts and leaves after. Keys travel.
- **Let the consequence show.** Typing should change something with every character. A selection should move what it selects.
- **Hold.** Give a finished state long enough to be read and enjoyed, then move. Contrast between fast and still is rhythm.
- **Transitions are part of the story.** Push through, wipe from the point of action, match a shape across the cut, zoom into a detail. A hard cut on a beat can be the best choice. A crossfade is rarely the most interesting one.
- **A higher frame rate helps** when motion is fast and precise. Sixty frames per second makes interface motion look noticeably more polished than thirty.
- **Use a camera** if it helps: a slow push, a tilt that settles, a pull-back that reveals scale. Keep text readable when it stops.

## Sound

Sound is half of how a piece feels, and it is worth doing even for work that will mostly be seen muted.

- **Music follows picture, or picture follows music.** Find the turns in the video (the moment the product arrives, the quiet moment, the final image) and make the music change there: something enters, something drops out, something resolves. Or cut the picture to an existing track's structure. Either way the two should agree.
- **Give it dynamics.** A quiet opening, a fuller middle, a breath before the end. A track at one level throughout flattens the picture.
- **Small sounds on real actions.** A soft tick for a keystroke, a low thock for a press, a swell under a transition, a rising tone under a process. One sound per visible action, and none for decoration.
- **Where it comes from.** A track the person supplies, a licensed library, or one synthesised in code from oscillators and noise, which has no licensing questions and can be timed to the frame. Whatever the source, be sure the use is permitted.
- **Keep the silent version and the audio separately**, so sound can be replaced without rendering again.
- **Be honest about hearing.** If you cannot listen to the result, you can still check its length, its loudness and where its sections change. Say that is all you checked.

## Making it

Use whatever you have. Anything that can draw a frame as a function of time works: a React video framework, plain HTML and CSS captured frame by frame, canvas, SVG, a 3D engine, a scripting layer over a traditional editor. The guidance above does not depend on the tool.

Habits that help in any of them:

- **Keep every word in one place**, so the copy can be checked against the real product in a single read.
- **Keep timing in one place too**: the order and length of moments, and where sound should land. If picture and sound both read from it, they stay in sync through edits.
- **Think in a unit that is easy to reason about** (seconds, or a fixed number of beats per second) and convert to frames at the edge.
- **Build a small vocabulary** of reusable motions (an entrance, a press, a typed string, an eased path) and compose from it. Consistency across the piece comes from reuse.
- **Make variation deterministic.** Random values that differ between frames or render passes cause flicker.
- **Let the timeline own transitions** between moments, so individual scenes do not also fade themselves.
- **Design for the real frame size.** A vertical or square version deserves its own layout, not a crop.

## Look at what you made

A render you have not looked at is not finished.

- Render something fast and small, pull stills from it at several points in every moment (mid-entrance, the payoff, the hold, inside each transition), and actually look at them. Tiling a few frames into one image makes this quick.
- In those stills, look for: anything cut off or overlapping; text too small to read at half size; captions that wrap or collide; elements that all arrive at once; large empty areas that were not intended; a frame with nothing happening after a payoff; two moments composed identically in a row; anything that does not match the real product.
- Fix, render again, look again. Two passes is a minimum. Fresh eyes help: if you can get an independent review of the frames, take it.
- Stills cannot tell you about rhythm, smoothness at speed, or sound. Know that, and ask the person to watch it once at full speed.

**When someone says it looks basic**, the cause is usually in this list: the whole interface never leaves the screen; time is divided evenly; everything fades in and nothing has weight; type is one style throughout; the stage is busy while the subject is plain; there is no single idea. Find which, say so, and rebuild around the fix instead of adding decoration on top.

## The one fixed point

A video about a real product is a claim about it.

- Show what the product actually says and does. Take labels, shortcuts, feature names, numbers and the tagline from the source. If you are unsure whether something exists, check or leave it out.
- If the person's own setup differs from the public default, show the default and mention it.
- Recordings of a real screen often contain private things: credentials, links, names, messages, other applications. Look before using them, tell the person what you found, and do not reproduce it.
- Use only logos, quotes, music and footage there is a right to use.
- When you hand it over, say what you verified and what you could not, which parts are illustration, and which content is invented.

Everything else is yours to decide.
